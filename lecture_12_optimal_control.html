What Lecture 12 is About

Optimal Control Theory: Continuous-Time Dynamic Optimization

Optimal control theory is the continuous-time analogue of dynamic programming. Instead of a sequence of discrete decisions, the agent chooses a function u(t) over a time interval [0,T] to maximize an integral of payoffs, subject to a differential equation governing how the state x(t) evolves.

The Vehicle Analogy (from Bewley)
Imagine driving an all-terrain vehicle from point x₀ to point x₁. At each moment t you choose direction and throttle u(t) ∈ U. Your position x(t) evolves according to dx/dt = g(x(t),u(t)) (different terrain = different g). Fuel consumption rate = f(x(t),u(t)). Goal: minimize total fuel ∫f dt. This is an optimal control problem.

Section 1

The Optimal Control Problem

Definition: Piecewise Continuous Function
A function u : [0,T] → U is piecewise continuous if:
  1. u is continuous except at a finite number of values of t, and
  2. at each discontinuity t̄, both the left-hand limit lim_{t→t̄, t<t̄} u(t) and the right-hand limit lim_{t→t̄, t>t̄} u(t) exist.
The set of admissible controls: 𝒜 = {u : [0,T] → U | T > 0 and u is piecewise continuous}. The control set U is a fixed closed subset of ℝᴷ; the end time T may be variable.
Non-Examples of Piecewise Continuity
  • u(t) = 1/(1−t) for 0≤t<1, u(t)=1 for t≥1 — NOT piecewise continuous because lim_{t→1⁻} u(t) = +∞ doesn't exist (finite)
  • u(t) = sin(t⁻¹) for 0<t≤1, u(0)=0 — NOT piecewise continuous because lim_{t→0⁺} u(t) doesn't exist
The General Problem (Equation 12.1 in the notes)
The Optimal Control Problem
max_{u ∈ 𝒜} ∫₀ᵀ f(x(t), u(t)) dt subject to: dx(t)/dt = g(x(t), u(t)), for all t ∈ [0,T] x(0) = x₀ (initial condition) x(T) = x₁ (terminal condition)

The functions f : ℝᴺ × U → ℝ (instantaneous payoff) and gₙ : ℝᴺ × U → ℝ for n=1,…,N (state dynamics) are assumed continuously differentiable in x and continuous in u.

State Variable x(t)

Describes the system at time t. Evolves according to the differential equation dx/dt = g(x,u). Examples: capital stock K(t), position x(t), pollution stock P(t).

Control Variable u(t)

The decision at each moment t. Chosen from the set U. Examples: consumption C(t), investment rate I(t), velocity and direction of vehicle.

Why Continuous Time?
Many economic models are cleaner in continuous time: interest compounds continuously, capital depreciates continuously, and the Hamiltonian conditions are often more tractable than their discrete analogues. The Euler equation in discrete time becomes the costate evolution equation dλ/dt = −∂H/∂x in continuous time.
Section 2

Deriving Necessary Conditions via Discrete Approximation and Kuhn-Tucker

Bewley derives the optimal control conditions in a non-rigorous but highly illuminating way: approximate the continuous-time problem by a discrete-time one with M time steps, apply the Kuhn-Tucker theorem, then take the limit as M→∞.

The Discrete Approximation (Equation 12.2)
Divide [0,T] into M intervals of length Δt = T/M. The problem becomes:
max Σₘ₌₀ᴹ⁻¹ Δt · f(x(mΔt), u(mΔt)) subject to: x(mΔt) − x((m−1)Δt) ≤ Δt · g(x((m−1)Δt), u((m−1)Δt)), m=1,…,M x(0) ≤ x₀ −x(MΔt) ≤ −x₁
The objective is a Riemann sum approximating ∫f dt. The constraints are inequalities (so Kuhn-Tucker applies).
Applying the Kuhn-Tucker Theorem

Since f is concave and the constraint functions are convex, the KT theorem applies. It yields KT multipliers λ(0),…,λ(M) and β ≥ 0 with complementary slackness conditions, and the vector of controls and states maximizes the Lagrangian L (equation 12.3 in the notes).

Summation by Parts (Discrete Integration by Parts)

To simplify L, Bewley uses the discrete analogue of integration by parts. Recall the continuous version:

∫₀ᵀ (dF/dt) G(t) dt = F(T)G(T) − F(0)G(0) − ∫₀ᵀ F(t)(dG/dt) dt

The discrete analogue (summation by parts) uses Leibniz's rule for first differences: Δ(xₘyₘ) = yₘ₊₁Δxₘ + xₘΔyₘ, which gives:

Σₘ₌₁ᴹ Δ(xₘyₘ) = x_{M+1}y_{M+1} − x₁y₁ = Σₘ yₘ₊₁Δxₘ + Σₘ xₘΔyₘ

After applying summation by parts to the Lagrangian, it simplifies to a sum over m of terms:

L = Σₘ₌₀ᴹ⁻¹ Δt [f(x(mΔt), u(mΔt)) + λ(m+1) g(x(mΔt), u(mΔt))] + Σₘ₌₀ᴹ⁻¹ x(mΔt) Δλ(m) + [β − λ(M)] x(T)
Taking the Limit as Δt → 0

Maximizing L over u(mΔt) gives (from 12.4): u(mΔt) maximizes f(x(mΔt),u) + λ(m+1)g(x(mΔt),u) over u ∈ U. Maximizing over x(mΔt) gives the first-order condition:

[λ(m+1)−λ(m)] / Δt = −∂/∂x [f(x(mΔt), u(mΔt)) + λ(m+1)g(x(mΔt), u(mΔt))]

Letting Δt → 0 (m → ∞ with mΔt → t), assuming λ is differentiable:

dλ(t)/dt = −∂/∂x [f(x(t), u(t)) + λ(t) g(x(t), u(t))]

And the constraint dx/dt = g(x,u) holds in the limit (constraints active at optimum). The control condition becomes: u(t) maximizes f(x(t),u) + λ(t)g(x(t),u) over u ∈ U for all t.

Section 3

The Hamiltonian Function and the Maximum Principle

Definition: The Hamiltonian Function
The Hamiltonian is defined as:
H(x, u, λ) = f(x, u) + λ g(x, u)
It is the continuous-time instantaneous analogue of the Lagrangian. It combines the instantaneous payoff f and the "value" of the state change g (weighted by the costate variable λ).
The Three Optimality Conditions (N = K = 1 case)
Let u(t) be the optimal control. For all t:
(i) u(t) = argmax_{u∈U} H(x(t), u, λ(t)) [Maximum Principle] (ii) dx(t)/dt = ∂H/∂λ = g(x(t), u(t)) [State equation] (iii) dλ(t)/dt = −∂H/∂x = −∂/∂x[f(x,u) + λg(x,u)] [Costate equation]
Conditions (ii) and (iii) together are called the Hamiltonian system. Condition (i) is the maximum principle.
Interpreting the Three Conditions
  • Maximum principle (i): At every instant t, choose the control u to maximize the Hamiltonian H — the immediate payoff plus the value of the state change.
  • State equation (ii): The state x evolves according to the system dynamics g. This is the "feasibility" constraint in differential form.
  • Costate equation (iii): λ(t) evolves to track how the future value of the problem changes with the state. It is the "marginal value of x" — the shadow price of the state variable.
Constancy of the Hamiltonian

The maximum principle implies ∂H/∂u = 0 (when H is differentiable in u and the interior of U contains the optimal u(t)). Then, differentiating H along the optimal path:

dH/dt = (∂H/∂x)(dx/dt) + (∂H/∂u)(du/dt) + (∂H/∂λ)(dλ/dt) = (∂H/∂x)(∂H/∂λ) + 0 + (∂H/∂λ)(−∂H/∂x) = 0
Constancy of H Along Optimal Path
H(x(t), u(t), λ(t)) = constant along the optimal trajectory, provided f and g do not depend directly on t. (If there is explicit time dependence, H is not necessarily constant.)
Economic Meaning
The constancy of H is the continuous-time analogue of the fact that the Lagrangian is constant at an optimum. In growth models, H = u(C) + λ[f(K)−C] constant means: the sum of current utility plus the value of investment is constant over time. This is a powerful constraint on optimal trajectories.
Example 12.1 — The Linear-Quadratic Problem
Problem
Bring a point from x(0)=x₀ to x(T)=0, minimizing ∫₀ᵀ [x²(t) + (dx/dt)²] dt. Here u(t) = dx/dt (velocity is the control), so g(x,u) = u and f(x,u) = −x² − u².
Solution via Euler's Equation
Since u = dx/dt, the Hamiltonian condition gives λ = −∂f/∂u = 2u = 2dx/dt, and the costate equation gives dλ/dt = −∂f/∂x = 2x. Eliminating λ:
d²x/dt² = x(t) for all t
General solution: x(t) = aeᵗ + be⁻ᵗ for constants a, b. Using boundary conditions x(0)=x₀ and x(T)=0:
a = −x₀/(e²ᵀ−1) and b = e²ᵀx₀/(e²ᵀ−1) x(t) = −[x₀/(e²ᵀ−1)]eᵗ + [e²ᵀx₀/(e²ᵀ−1)]e⁻ᵗ
Optimal Horizon T = ∞
The value function V(T) = x₀²/(e²ᵀ−1) + x₀²/(1−e⁻²ᵀ) is decreasing in T. So the optimal choice is T = ∞, giving solution x(t) = x₀e⁻ᵗ (a=0, b=x₀). The state decays exponentially to zero.
Example with No Solution
If there is no cost to changing x (no u² term): max ∫−x²(t)dt subject to x(0) given. The optimal thing is to reach 0 as fast as possible. But no matter how large |dx/dt|, you can always do better by going faster still. No finite-speed program is optimal → no solution exists (unless x(0)=0).
Section 4

The General Vector Case: N State Variables, K Controls

The Hamiltonian System (General Case)
Let x = (x₁,…,xₙ) ∈ ℝᴺ (state), u = (u₁,…,uₖ) ∈ U ⊆ ℝᴷ (control), λ = (λ₁,…,λₙ) ∈ ℝᴺ (costates). The Hamiltonian:
H(x, u, λ) = f(x, u) + Σₙ λₙ gₙ(x, u)
Necessary conditions for optimality: ∃ piecewise differentiable λ₁(t),…,λₙ(t) (not all zero) such that for all t:
dxₙ(t)/dt = ∂H/∂λₙ = gₙ(x(t), u(t)) [state equation, n=1,…,N] dλₙ(t)/dt = −∂H/∂xₙ [costate equation, n=1,…,N] u(t) = argmax_{u∈U} H(x(t), u, λ(t)) [maximum principle] dH/dt = 0 along optimal path [constancy of H]
State and Costate Variables
  • State variables x₁(t),…,xₙ(t): describe the system. Initial and terminal conditions x(0)=x₀, x(T)=x₁ are given.
  • Costate (dual/conjugate/auxiliary) variables λ₁(t),…,λₙ(t): the shadow prices of the state variables. They satisfy the costate ODE and need NOT be non-negative (unlike KT multipliers).
Determining the Solution
The Hamiltonian system is a system of 2N ODEs in (x₁,…,xₙ,λ₁,…,λₙ), requiring 2N initial conditions. These are provided by the N initial conditions x(0)=x₀ and N terminal conditions x(T)=x₁. Given (x(t),λ(t)), the optimal u(t) is determined by the maximum principle at each t.
Euler's Equation as a Special Case
When the control is the rate of change of the state (u = dx/dt, so g(x,u) = u), the maximum principle gives λ = −∂f/∂u and the costate equation gives dλ/dt = −∂f/∂x. Eliminating λ gives:
d/dt [∂f(x, dx/dt)/∂(dx/dt)] = ∂f(x, dx/dt)/∂x
This is the classical Euler equation from the calculus of variations, now recovered as a special case of the maximum principle.
Section 5

The Value Function in Optimal Control and the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman Equation

The Value Function

The value function at time 0 is V(x₀, 0) = max over admissible controls of ∫₀ᵀ f(x(t),u(t)) dt, starting at x(0)=x₀. More generally, the value function at time t is:

V(x, t) = max ∫ₜᵀ f(x(s), u(s)) ds s.t. dx(s)/ds = g(x(s),u(s)), x(t)=x, x(T)=x₁
Costate Variable as Derivative of Value Function
If V(x,t) is differentiable in x, then:
λ(t) = ∂V(x̄(t), t)/∂x
The costate variable at time t is the marginal value of the state variable — exactly as the Lagrange multiplier is the shadow price of the constraint in static optimization.
The Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) Equation

Using the fundamental theorem of calculus and the chain rule, Bewley derives:

−f(x(t), u(t)) = dV(x(t),t)/dt = [∂V/∂x][dx/dt] + ∂V/∂t = λ(t)g(x(t),u(t)) + ∂V/∂t

Rearranging and using the maximum principle:

Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman Equation
∂V(x(t), t)/∂t = − max_{u∈U} H(x(t), u, ∂V/∂x) = − max_{u∈U} [f(x(t), u) + (∂V/∂x) g(x(t), u)]
This is a partial differential equation in V(x,t). It is the continuous-time analogue of the Bellman equation in discrete time. Solving it over the whole state space gives V, from which the optimal control policy can be recovered.

Discrete-Time Bellman Equation

V(x₀) = max_{x₁∈G(x₀)} [u(x₀,x₁) + βV(x₁)]

A functional equation: V(x) appears on both sides.

Solved by contraction mapping / value function iteration.

Continuous-Time HJB Equation

∂V/∂t = −max_{u∈U} [f(x,u) + (∂V/∂x)g(x,u)]

A partial differential equation in V(x,t).

Solved analytically in some cases; numerically in others.

The "Guess and Verify" Method
For many economic models, one guesses a functional form for V(x,t) (e.g., V = A + B ln(K)) and verifies it satisfies the HJB. Substituting ∂V/∂x for λ(t) in the Hamiltonian system and finding constants A, B yields a complete solution. This is what Bewley does in the growth model example.
Section 6 — Appendix to Lecture 12

Application: Optimal Economic Growth Model

A single commodity used for either capital or consumption. Output = f(K(t)) (production function). Investment = dK/dt = f(K) − C (capital accumulation). Utility flow = u(C(t)).

The Optimal Growth Problem
max_{C:[0,T]→[0,∞)} ∫₀ᵀ u(C(t)) dt subject to: dK(t)/dt = f(K(t)) − C(t) [capital accumulation] K(0) = K₀ > 0 [initial capital] K(T) ≥ K₁ > 0 [terminal capital constraint]
State variable: K(t). Control variable: C(t) ≥ 0. Assumptions: u' > 0, u'' < 0, f' > 0, f'' < 0 (diminishing returns).
Applying the Hamiltonian
The Hamiltonian and Optimality Conditions
H(K, C, λ) = u(C) + λ[f(K) − C]

Hamiltonian system:

dK/dt = ∂H/∂λ = f(K(t)) − C(t) [state equation = capital accumulation] dλ/dt = −∂H/∂K = −λ(t) · f'(K(t)) [costate equation]

Maximum principle: C(t) maximizes H = u(C) + λ(t)[f(K)−C] over C ≥ 0. If C(t) > 0:

∂H/∂C = 0 → u'(C(t)) = λ(t) [marginal utility = shadow price of capital]
Interpreting u'(C) = λ
The costate variable λ(t) is the shadow price of capital — the marginal value of an additional unit of capital at time t. The condition u'(C) = λ says: at the optimum, the marginal utility of consumption equals the marginal value of investing (not consuming) that unit. This is the classic "equimarginal principle."
The Continuous-Time Euler Equation

From u'(C(t)) = λ(t) and the costate equation dλ/dt = −λ(t)f'(K(t)):

d/dt [u'(C(t))] = −u'(C(t)) · f'(K(t)) [equation 12.5]

This is the continuous-time analogue of the discrete-time Euler equation from Lecture 10:

u'(Cₜ) = β · u'(Cₜ₊₁) · F'(Kₜ) [discrete] d/dt [u'(C(t))] = −u'(C(t)) · f'(K(t)) [continuous, with no discounting]
Constancy of the Hamiltonian (Equation 12.6)
H = u(C(t)) + λ(t)[f(K(t))−C(t)] = constant. Using λ=u'(C) and dK/dt=f(K)−C:
u(C(t)) + u'(C(t)) · dK(t)/dt = constant (12.6)
Key Properties of Optimal Paths
If C(0) > 0 (so λ(0) > 0): since f' > 0, dλ/dt = −λf' < 0, so λ is decreasing. Since λ = u'(C) and u'' < 0, dC/dt = [u'']⁻¹ · dλ/dt > 0: consumption is always increasing. Also: when dK/dt=0, d²K/dt² < 0 (capital is at a local maximum when stationary).
Specific Functions: Quadratic Utility, Linear Production

Let u(C) = −(a/2)(C−C̄)² and f(K) = bK, where a, C̄, b > 0. Then:

H(K, C, λ) = −(a/2)(C−C̄)² + λ(bK−C)
Costate Evolution and Phase Space
The costate equation: dλ/dt = −bλ(t), so λ(t) = λ(0)e⁻ᵇᵗ. The constancy of H, combined with u'(C)=a(C̄−C), gives: K̇² − b²(K−K̄)² = γ, where K̄ = C̄/b (bliss capital stock) and γ is a constant.

This is an equation of conic sections in (K, K̇)-space (phase space):

  • γ = 0: Two straight lines through (K̄, 0): K̇ = ±b(K−K̄). Optimal paths lie on these lines.
  • γ ≠ 0: Hyperbolas with asymptotes K̇ = ±b(K−K̄). Optimal paths lie on branches of these hyperbolas.
The Turnpike Property
As the time horizon T → ∞, the optimal path spends most of its time near K̄ (the bliss capital stock). The path enters from K₀, approaches K̄ closely, lingers there for most of the planning period, then exits toward K₁. This property — that optimal long-run paths concentrate near a "turnpike" — is a general feature of optimal growth models. The term "turnpike" refers to the resemblance of the (K,t)-graph to a highway map with entrance/exit ramps.

From the phase diagram (K, K̇)-space (Figures 12.3–12.5 in the notes):

  • Paths further from the K-axis (higher |K̇|) move faster, reaching K₁ sooner (smaller T).
  • The path labeled 1 in the notes reaches K₁ fastest; paths 2, 3, 4 take progressively longer by overshooting K̄.
  • Paths that overshoot spend a long time near K̄ (slow dynamics near the bliss point), spending a fraction → 1 of total time near K̄ as T → ∞.
Lecture 13

Optimal Control Theory II: Deriving the HJB from the Bellman Equation

Bewley shows the connection between the Bellman equation (discrete time) and the HJB equation (continuous time) by deriving the latter from the former via a limiting argument.

Derivation of the HJB from the Bellman Equation

Let (x̄(t), ū(t)) be optimal. Think of dt as a small positive number. By the discrete Bellman equation and Taylor's theorem:

V(x̄(t), t) = max_{u∈U} [f(x̄(t), u) dt + V(x̄(t+dt), t+dt)] + o(dt)

Expanding V(x̄(t+dt), t+dt) by Taylor:

V(x̄(t+dt), t+dt) ≈ V(x̄(t), t) + (∂V/∂x)(dx̄/dt)dt + (∂V/∂t)dt = V(x̄(t),t) + (∂V/∂x)g(x̄,u)dt + (∂V/∂t)dt

Substituting and canceling V(x̄(t),t) from both sides:

0 = (∂V/∂t)dt + max_{u∈U} [f(x̄(t),u) + (∂V/∂x)g(x̄(t),u)] dt + o(dt)

Dividing by dt and letting dt → 0:

Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman Equation (Equation 13.1)
∂V(x(t), t)/∂t = − max_{u∈U} [f(x(t), u) + (∂V(x(t),t)/∂x) · g(x(t), u)]
This PDE, combined with the terminal condition V(x,T) = 0 (or the appropriate boundary condition), fully characterizes the value function. Solving it is both necessary and sufficient for optimality, provided V is differentiable.
Verification: Maximum Principle from HJB
Substituting λ(t) = ∂V/∂x into the HJB and using the maximum principle (ū(t) maximizes H), we recover:
∂V/∂t = −f(x(t), ū(t)) − λ(t)g(x(t), ū(t))
This confirms that the maximum principle is consistent with the HJB — the HJB provides an alternative derivation of the maximum principle when V is differentiable.

Why Optimal Control Appears in PhD Econ
  • Trade: Dynamic models of firm learning, technology adoption, and export entry use optimal control (x = knowledge stock, u = investment in R&D).
  • Macro: Ramsey-Cass-Koopmans growth model is an optimal control problem. The costate λ = marginal utility of consumption.
  • IO: Dynamic pricing, capacity investment, patent races.
  • International Econ: Optimal tariff dynamics, debt accumulation, exchange rate paths.
